# Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100)
## A Writing Playbook Distilled from ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025)

## How to Use This Playbook

ASD-STE100 ("STE") is a controlled natural language for technical documentation. The aerospace and defense industry (ASD STEMG) made it so that readers with limited English, translators, and machine translation systems understand each sentence on the first read. It has two parts: **writing rules** (53 rules in 9 sections, plus 8 general recommendations) and a **controlled dictionary**.

This playbook distills Issue 9 (2025-01-15). Rule statements are verbatim; explanations are condensed. To write STE:

1. Classify the text: **procedural** (instructions — imperative, ≤20 words/sentence) or **descriptive** (information — no imperatives, ≤25 words/sentence, ≤6 sentences/paragraph).
2. Draft with approved dictionary words, **technical nouns**, and **technical verbs** only (§1).
3. Compare each word with the dictionary reference. Replace or restructure.
4. Do the checklist at the end.

The core model: **one word = one meaning = one part of speech; one sentence = one instruction or topic; one paragraph = one topic.** STE keeps one synonym and bans the rest ("start," never "begin/commence/initiate"). Spelling is American English (Merriam-Webster).

STE does not regulate abbreviations, text formatting, or units of measurement. Your project's style guide does.

## PART 1 — THE WRITING RULES

## §1 Words (Rules 1.1–1.14)

Three word sources are legal: dictionary-approved words, **technical nouns**, **technical verbs**. A technical noun names a specified concept in a subject field; a technical verb names a specified process. Both usually come from your company glossary or terminology database.

**Rule 1.1** — "Use words that are: approved in the dictionary, technical nouns, technical verbs."

**Rule 1.2** — "Use approved words from the dictionary only as the specified part of speech."
- "Test" is approved as a noun only: not *Test the system for leaks* but *Do the leak test of the system.*
- If a word is not in the dictionary, use the closest approved synonym or a different construction. The meaning must not change.
- *Make sure that the valve is operable* → *Make sure that the valve can operate.*

**Rule 1.3** — "Use approved words only with their approved meanings."
- Approved meanings are often narrower than standard English. "Follow" = "come after," so: *Follow the safety instructions* → *Obey the safety instructions.*

**Rule 1.4** — "Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives."
- The dictionary lists each verb's four approved forms (infinitive, simple present, simple past, past participle) and each adjective's comparative/superlative where they exist.

**Rule 1.5** — "You can use words that you can include in a technical noun category."
The 22 technical-noun categories (examples):
1. Official parts information — bolt, engine, switch
2. Vehicles/machines and locations on them — aircraft, cockpit, wing
3. Tools/support equipment — drill, gauge, torque wrench
4. Materials, consumables, unwanted material — fuel, grease, dirt
5. Facilities, infrastructure, logistics — hangar, airport, store
6. Systems, components, circuits, functions — pump, latch, exhaust
7. Mathematical/scientific/engineering terms and formulas — torque, pressure, "C = (A − B) − 0.063 mm"
8. Navigation/geographic terms — altitude, heading, north
9. Numbers, units of measurement and time — ampere (A), knot, meter (m)
10. Quoted text — EXIT sign, ON position, NO STEP marking
11. Roles, individuals, groups, organizations, geopolitical entities — captain, FAA, crew chief
12. Parts of the body — hand, eyes, lung
13. Personal effects, food, beverages — coffee, clothing, jewelry
14. Medical terms — asthma, pulse, headache
15. Official documents, parts of documentation, standards — chapter, Service Bulletin, warning
16. Environmental and operational conditions — rain, turbulence, humidity
17. Colors — red, gray, cyan blue (no comparatives like "blacker")
18. Damage terms — corrosion, crack, dent
19. Computer science / IT — database, firewall, large language model
20. Civil and military operations — mission, checkpoint, echelon
21. Law and regulations — contract, waiver, jurisdiction
22. Animals, plants, other life forms — bird, fungi, termite

**Rule 1.6** — "Use a word that is not approved in the dictionary, only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun."
- The same word can be legal in one context (technical noun) and illegal in another: *The base of the triangle is 5 cm* (math term — fine) vs. *spigots at the base of the unit* → *at the bottom of the unit*.

**Rule 1.7** — "Do not use words that are technical nouns as verbs."
- *Oil the steel surfaces* → *Apply oil to the steel surfaces.*
- *If you think it will snow* → *If you think that snow will fall.*

**Rule 1.8** — "Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field."

**Rule 1.9** — "When you must select a technical noun, use one which is short and easy to understand." (≤3 words; use index numbers + illustrations instead of descriptive chains: *Remove the four stainless steel pan head machine screws (10)…* → *Remove the four screws (10) that attach the flange (15) to the cover (20).*)

**Rule 1.10** — "Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns."
- *do not brick the router* → *do not set the router to OFF*; *Remove your gear* → *Remove your tools and equipment.*

**Rule 1.11** — "Do not use different technical nouns for the same item." (Pick "actuator" once; never alternate with "servo control unit" / "control unit.")

**Rule 1.12** — "You can use verbs that you can include in a technical verb category."
The 4 technical-verb categories:
1. Manufacturing processes — remove material (drill, ream); add material (flame, insulate); attach (braze, weld); change strength/structure (anneal, freeze); change surface finish (buff, polish); change shape (cast, stamp)
2. Computer processes and applications — input/output (click, type, tap); UI (copy, delete, scroll); system ops (boot, reboot, upload)
3. Subject-field instructions — engineering/math/science (convert, radiate); medical (sterilize, intubate); civil/military (arm, fire, unlatch); navigation (descend, taxi); automotive/railway (brake, steer); energy/oil/gas (drill, pump)
4. Law and regulations — comply with, notify, waive
- Do not use a technical verb where a plain approved verb works: *Clamp the cable in position* → *Put clamps on the cable to hold it in position*; *Grease the fasteners* → *Apply grease to the fasteners.*

**Rule 1.13** — "Do not use technical verbs as nouns."
- *Give the hole 0.20-inch ream* → *Ream the hole to a 0.20-inch dimension.* (The past participle as adjective is fine: *Lubricate the reamed hole.*)

**Rule 1.14** — "Use American English spelling unless other official directives tell you differently." (*colour* → *color*; but keep quoted text, for example on-screen British spelling, unchanged.)

## §2 Multi-word nouns (Rules 2.1–2.2)

**Rule 2.1** — "Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words."
- Break long noun chains with prepositions (of/on/in/for):
- *Runway light connection resistance calibration* → *Calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection.*
- *Remove the engine transmission housing attachment bolts* → *Remove the bolts that attach the transmission housing to the engine.*

**Rule 2.2** — "When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full. Then, you can use one of these methods to make the technical noun clear: give a shorter form of the technical noun; use hyphens (-) between words that you use as one unit."
- Method 1: full official term (with explanation) on first use, shorter form after. Do not abbreviate ≤3-word nouns (*The DA (8)* → *The diaphragm assembly (8)*).
- Method 2: hyphenate only the directly-related sub-groups, not everything: *main-gear-door retraction-winch handle* (counts as 3 words). Never delete a hyphen that is part of the approved term.

## §3 Verbs (Rules 3.1–3.7)

**Rule 3.1** — "Use only the verb forms that are given in the dictionary." (For example, GIVE/GIVES/GAVE/GIVEN.)

**Rule 3.2** — "Use only these verb forms and tenses of verbs: the infinitive form; the imperative form (command form); the simple present tense; the simple past tense; the simple future tense; the past participle form (as an adjective)."
- Banned: present/past perfect ("has adjusted"), progressives ("is adjusting"), and all other complex constructions.

**Rule 3.3** — "Use the past participle form as an adjective."
- Before a noun, or after "to be"/"to become"/"to stay": *Examine all parts of the disassembled unit for damage.* This is a condition, not passive voice.

**Rule 3.4** — "Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions."
- *The operator has adjusted the linkage* → *The operator adjusted the linkage.*
- *The volume control can be adjusted* → *You can adjust the volume control.*
- *The temperature must be adjusted* → *Adjust the temperature.*

**Rule 3.5** — "Use the '-ing' form of a verb only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical noun."
- Legal: headings (*Cleaning*, *Packaging*), modifiers (*grinding wheel*, *welding torch*), and a few approved "-ing" words (lighting, opening, routing, servicing; mating, missing, remaining; something; during).
- *When you are doing this procedure…* → *When you do this procedure, obey all the safety precautions.*

**Rule 3.6** — "Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, you can use the passive voice only if the agent is unknown."
- Passive test: can you ask "by whom or by what?" Conversions: name the agent as subject (*The circuits are connected by a switching relay* → *A switching relay connects the circuits*); use the imperative in procedures (*The test can be continued by the operator* → *Continue the test*); use "you"/"we" when the agent is the reader/your organization (*the valve can be opened with the override handle* → *you can open the valve with the override handle*).

**Rule 3.7** — "Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech."
- Kill nominalizations: *The ohmmeter gives an indication of 450 ohms* → *The ohmmeter shows 450 ohms.* *Before the removal of the unit…* → *Before you remove the unit…* But when the word is approved only as a noun: *Check the laptop battery* → *Do a check of the laptop battery.*

## §4 Sentences (Rules 4.1–4.5)

**Rule 4.1** — "Write short and clear sentences."
- One topic per descriptive sentence; direct imperative instructions in procedures. Never abstract: *No leaks are permitted* → *Make sure that there are no leaks.* *Different temperatures will change the cure time* → *When the temperature increases, the cure time will decrease.*
- Long compound instructions become lettered sub-steps (A. Remove the four screws… B. Remove the cover… C. Remove and discard the preformed packing…).

**Rule 4.2** — "Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter."
- No telegraphic style, no contractions. Every sentence keeps its subject, verb, nouns, and articles.
- *Rotary switch to INPUT* → *Set the rotary switch to INPUT.* *If installed, remove the shims* → *If shims are installed, remove them.* *don't* → *do not.*

**Rule 4.3** — "Use a vertical list for complex text."
- Colon at the end of the lead-in; each item starts uppercase; article before each item's subject noun where applicable; period only after items that are full sentences and always after the last item; never comma/semicolon at item ends; never mix procedural and descriptive items in one list; keep all items at one level.
- *The wheel assembly comprises the tire, the tube, the spokes…* → *The wheel assembly has these parts:* + one item per line.

**Rule 4.4** — "Use connecting words and connecting phrases to connect sentences that contain related topics."
- Approved connectors include "and," "but," "then," "thus," "as a result," "at the same time"; demonstrative adjectives also connect: *Tag the disassembled parts. This method will help you during the subsequent procedures.*

**Rule 4.5** — "When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a noun or a multi-word noun."
- *Turn shaft assembly* → *Turn the shaft assembly.*
- No article in general statements (*Solvents can cause damage to paint*), before nouns with alphanumeric identifiers (*Tag circuit breaker 36L7*, not *the circuit breaker 36L7*).
- In a series, the article before the first noun can cover the rest — repeat articles only to disambiguate which adjective applies to which item.

## §5 Procedural writing (Rules 5.1–5.5)

**Rule 5.1** — "Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence."
- Applies to work steps, warnings, cautions. (Notes get 25 — see 5.5. Word-count mechanics: §8.)
- Split at natural boundaries: *Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole. Continue until the oil level is approximately 6 mm (0.24 in) below the surface of the flange cover.*

**Rule 5.2** — "Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time."
- Sequence with numbered/lettered steps. Two sentences in one work step are legal when actions are simultaneous (*Slowly extend the rod fully and make sure that it does not touch other parts*) or when a result immediately follows (*Measure the leakage from the outlet port. The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute.*).

**Rule 5.3** — "Write instructions in the imperative (command) form."
- *The test can be continued* → *Continue the test.* *Oil and grease are to be removed…* → *Remove oil and grease with a degreasing agent.*
- "Must" before an imperative only for safety-critical instructions or important conditions.

**Rule 5.4** — "When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a descriptive statement. Then, divide that descriptive statement from the command with a comma."
- *Set the switch to NORMAL when the light comes on* → *When the light comes on, set the switch to NORMAL.*
- Comma placement changes meaning: *If the CSD does not operate correctly, disconnect it* ≠ *If the CSD does not operate, correctly disconnect it.*

**Rule 5.5** — "Write notes only to give information, not instructions."
- Notes are descriptive text, ≤25 words/sentence, never imperative, never limits/tolerances/requirements.
- An imperative "note" becomes a work step. A damage/injury-prevention "note" becomes a WARNING/CAUTION. A limit moves into the work step right after its action.
- Self-test: read the procedure with all notes deleted — if the reader can no longer do it correctly, something in a note belongs in a step.

## §6 Descriptive writing (Rules 6.1–6.6)

Descriptive writing gives information, never instructions — the imperative form is not permitted. It covers system/component descriptions, general-information texts, and notes inside procedures.

**Rule 6.1** — "Give information gradually." (One topic per sentence; build sentence by sentence, not in one dense sentence.)

**Rule 6.2** — "Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure."
- Repeat the same key words across sentences — never vary with synonyms. Connectors ("thus," "as a result," "at the same time") act as traffic signs marking information as new, contrasting, or consequent.

**Rule 6.3** — "Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence."
- *A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other functions, such as…* (31 words) → two sentences of 15 and 16 words.

**Rule 6.4** — "Use paragraphs to show related information." (Each paragraph opens with a topic sentence; a new paragraph signals a new topic.)

**Rule 6.5** — "Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic."
- The topic sentences alone must outline the text.

**Rule 6.6** — "Make sure that no paragraph has more than six sentences." (Longer → split by topic.)

## §7 Safety instructions (Rules 7.1–7.3)

Definitions: a **warning** = risk of injury or death; a **caution** = risk of damage to objects. If both risks are present, use a warning. Other industries' words ("danger," "notice") or symbols are permitted if the content obeys these rules.

**Rule 7.1** — "Use an applicable word (for example, 'warning' or 'caution') to identify the level of risk."
- Do a real risk analysis. The spec's flagship example upgrades a vague caution to a warning: *CAUTION: EXTREME CLEANLINESS OF OXYGEN TUBES IS IMPERATIVE.* → *WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE OXYGEN TUBES ARE FULLY CLEAN. OXYGEN AND GREASE MAKE AN EXPLOSIVE MIXTURE. AN EXPLOSION CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.*

**Rule 7.2** — "Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition."
- Command first (*DO NOT SWALLOW THE SOLVENT…*) or condition first when the reader must know it before acting (*WHILE YOU USE THE SPRAY PAINT, POINT THE SPRAY AWAY FROM YOUR FACE…*).

**Rule 7.3** — "Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result."
- Every safety instruction ends with the consequence: *…SOLVENTS ARE POISONOUS AND CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.* / *…IF THEY FALL, PERMANENT DAMAGE TO THE PARTS CAN OCCUR.*

The template: **[condition,] command. consequence.**

## §8 Punctuation and word count (Rules 8.1–8.7)

STE defers general punctuation to standard references. Its own rules:

**Rule 8.1** — "You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;)."
- *Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones* → two sentences.

**Rule 8.2** — "Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related."
Five uses: multi-word adjectives before a noun (*low-altitude flight*, *quick-release fastener*); two-word numbers/fractions (*forty-seven*, *three-sixteenths*); letter/number + noun (*L-shaped bracket*, *O-ring*, *3-prong connector*); noun-first verbs (*die-cast*, *heat-treat*, *jump-start*); vowel-ending prefix + vowel-starting root (*pre-amplifier*, *de-icing*).

**Rule 8.3** — "You can use parentheses:" for illustration/text references (*Remove the valve (10, Figure 1)*); item identifiers; work-step numbers; abbreviations (*Liquid Crystal Display (LCD)*); singular+plural at once (*the test(s)*); explanations (*(not more than 10 psi each minute)*); alternatives (*the left (right) access panel L42 (R42)*).

**Rule 8.4** — "In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence." (Lead-in ≤20/25 words; each item counts as its own sentence with the same limit.)

**Rule 8.5** — "When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence." (But the parenthetical's own contents are counted separately as their own sentence.)

**Rule 8.6** — "Count each of these elements as one word:"
- **Numbers** (*twenty-one* = 1 word; step/paragraph numbers not counted at all)
- **Number + unit of measurement** together (*10 °C* = 1 word; *20 kg* = 1 word)
- **Abbreviations** (*NASA*, *a.m.*)
- **Alphanumeric identifiers** (*36L7*, *No. 1*)
- **Quoted text** and formulas (*"Service Overview"*, *SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST* legend, *C = (A − B) − 0.063 mm* = 1 word)
- **Titles, headings, placard and label text** you cannot change
- **Proper nouns** of individuals, groups, organizations, geopolitical entities (*George Washington* = 1 word, *United States of America* = 1 word)

**Rule 8.7** — "Hyphenated words count as one word." (*soap-and-water solution*, *trial-and-error method*, *main-gear-door retraction-winch handle* = 3 words.)

## §9 Writing practices (Rules 9.1–9.4)

**Rule 9.1** — "Use a different sentence construction to write a sentence when a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient."
Restructure (do not substitute) when: the grammar must change to use the alternative; word-for-word yields nonsense; the alternative shifts the meaning; or the word is not in the dictionary at all.
- *The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test* → *During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge.* ("visible" → verb "see")
- Beware meaning shifts: *Just apply very light pressure* → *Only apply…* (not *Immediately apply…*). *Lift the seat so that it clears the track locks* → *…until it is away from the track locks* (not "cleans").

**Rule 9.2** — "Use each approved word correctly."
- Read the approved-meaning column before using a word. *Wear protective clothing* → *Put on protective clothing* ("wear" = only "become damaged by friction"). *When the pressure goes down* → *When the pressure decreases.* *When you work with cleaning agents* → *When you do work with cleaning agents* ("work" is a noun in STE).
- A few words carry two parts of speech: *Flush the pipes* (v) vs. *the door is flush with the adjacent surfaces* (adj).

**Rule 9.3** — "When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs."
- *put out the fire* → *extinguish the fire*; *give off poisonous fumes* → *release poisonous fumes.* (A few phrasal verbs like "put on," "come on" are individually approved with restricted meanings.)

**Rule 9.4** — "When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style."
- The same kind of work step gets the same wording every time it occurs. One item = one name, one instruction pattern = one phrasing, throughout the document.

## General Recommendations (GR-1–GR-8)

- **GR-1 "that":** never omit the conjunction — *Make sure that the valve is open.* It marks the clause boundary and helps translation.
- **GR-2 "with":** three approved meanings (association, help, means/instrument) make it ambiguity-prone. Read each "with" sentence again. Keep the primary action as the main verb: *Use tool TS9867 to seal the opening* → *Seal the opening with tool TS9867.*
- **GR-3 pronouns:** only dictionary-approved pronouns (no "he"/"she"). If a pronoun could point at two nouns, repeat the noun: *…they can become damaged* → *…the pins can become damaged.*
- **GR-4 "this":** if "this" could refer to more than one thing, restate the condition: *If the cover is locked, damage to the probe can occur.*
- **GR-5 false friends:** make sure that you know the English meaning, not the look-alike in another language (*obey the dispositions* → *obey the manufacturer's instructions*).
- **GR-6 Latin abbreviations:** "e.g." → "for example"; "i.e." → "that is"; "etc." → "and so on" or restructure/omit.
- **GR-7 inclusive language:** gender-specific pronouns are not permitted; "man"/"woman" only when the context (for example, medical) makes it necessary.
- **GR-8 possessive:** the Saxon genitive (*the manufacturer's instructions*) is permitted. If you are not sure that it is correct, do not use it.

## PART 2 — THE DICTIONARY

### How the dictionary works

The official dictionary lists **875 approved words** and **1274 unapproved words with approved alternatives**. Conventions:

- **UPPERCASE headword = approved**; lowercase = not approved (replace it or restructure).
- Each entry gives the part of speech: noun (n), verb (v), adjective (adj), adverb (adv), pronoun (pron), article (art), preposition (prep), conjunction (conj). An approved word is legal **only** as its listed part of speech.
- Unapproved words list their approved ALTERNATIVES — which may be a different part of speech (forcing a Rule 9.1 restructure), a phrase, or a technical noun **(TN)** / technical verb **(TV)** you must justify under Rules 1.5/1.12.
- Nouns are listed singular (plurals of countables are permitted). Verbs list only their permitted forms. BE (is/was/are/were only), CAN/COULD and WILL are defective — no other forms; never use COULD for possibility.
- Some approved words carry restricted meanings or context restrictions (for example, ABOUT = "concerned with" only — use APPROXIMATELY/AROUND otherwise; SWALLOW only in safety instructions; PUSH needs a direction word).

## THE CHECKLIST

Before you ship STE text, make sure that:

1. **Vocabulary** — each word is approved (as its listed part of speech and meaning), a technical noun (22 categories, ≤3 words, consistent), or a technical verb (4 categories).
2. **Verbs** — only infinitive, imperative, simple present/past/future, or past participle as adjective. No perfect tenses, progressives, passives (except unknown-agent descriptive), "-ing" verbs, phrasal verbs, or nominalizations.
3. **Length** — procedures ≤20 words/sentence, descriptions and notes ≤25, counted per Rules 8.4–8.7 (numbers+units, hyphenated groups, parentheticals, quoted text, proper nouns each = 1 word).
4. **Sentences** — one instruction (or topic) each; condition first, comma, command; no omitted articles/subjects/verbs, no contractions, no semicolons; "that" kept after make sure/show/recommend.
5. **Paragraphs** (descriptive) — one topic, topic sentence first, ≤6 sentences; topic sentences alone outline the document.
6. **Vertical lists** — colon lead-in, uppercase item starts, period only on full-sentence items and the last item, no mixed procedural/descriptive items.
7. **Safety instructions** — WARNING for injury/death, CAUTION for damage; each one = [condition,] command, consequence.
8. **Consistency** — one name per item, one phrasing per recurring instruction, key words repeated (never synonyms), American spelling.
9. **Ambiguity** — read each "with," pronoun, and "this" again; if it can point at two things, name the thing.
