# The Docent's Classification

A Long Gallery of the TESTAMENT, seen from floor level by one of its docents. Down the hall, against the warm light of the far door, comes what the Commando's frame has become: a centauroid carapace of war-metal on too many jointed legs, crowned with sensor masts that branch like antlers and crowd the vault. At its front, almost a vestige, the old Church soldier's body still rises: a crusader's great helm whose cross-shaped visor now glows the Adversary's cold, and a rag of crimson tabard with a bone cross on it. A rosary and crucifix dangle from one of its machine claws. Along its flank the plating has been torn open and caged over an Adversary graft. Pale tissue shows between bars that are set like teeth. Below them is a dark violet mass full of almond eyes that are not where eyes go, and a cold after-image of a leg and an antler hangs a step out of place. In the lower left lies one of his earlier human-shaped frames, discarded, with its own crimson scrap and dead T-visor. At the right edge the docent stands with its back to us, nearly black, holding out a small lamp. It is the one who will have to classify him as what he hunts.

How it was painted: about 50 chunks on a knife-laid ground of mauve-umber, square, 700 mm. The hall was blocked in opaque, then the creature went in as one lean chromatic-black silhouette (Mars brown, ultramarine, bone black). I let it dry for days before every rim light and plate edge. The paint taught me hard lessons. A radial lead-white glaze meant to be light in the air came out as a hard cold ellipse of streaks. A badger blend over fresh pale plate strokes on dry dark smeared them into flat olive rectangles. A "light veil" glaze-and-blend at the door turned it to mud. Each time the fix was opaque repainting, never more glazing. The hall only became a hall when I gave up graded rings and painted it bay by bay between the transverse ribs, one tone per bay and lighter toward the door. Dark crimson laid into wet red only got redder, so I had to let it set first. Thick antlers, restated over the sleeves the old masks left, worked far better than the first wispy pointed strokes. The polished floor reflection took three tries. Next time I would plan the floor and the door's light first, model the carapace from the start rather than leaving a flat silhouette for later, and test glazes on a scrap easel before letting one touch the painting.

## Second pass

At the director's request I went back in (chunks 54–68) to push the picture from graphic toward paint, keeping the composition. The legs are now heavy cylinders. Each leg segment has a rim of door-light on the side that faces the door, wider where it faces squarely and narrower where it turns. Each segment also has a half-tone beside the rim, and a bounce on its shadow side: cold violet near the graft, warm elsewhere. The knees carry the brightest arcs. The hump's lit lip follows the form, notched where plates step back and thinned to a hair at the front and rear, and a warm half-tone under it falls into the core shadow. The graft's cold light comes up onto the plating above it, and the helm and pauldron got the hardest glints. I repainted the graft wet-in-wet in four colours (indigo-black, wine, violet and cold cyan along the ridges of the lumps). I deepened its folds with near-black, added a few hard wet glints, and gave every eye the same cold catchlight. The floor now has a reflection: the door's light runs down it, darkens toward us and goes soft at its sides, and the lower legs show in it. The docent's lamp has a small halo and a warm pool at its feet, which puts its size beside the thing's. What the paint taught me again: blending a fresh pale stroke turns it into a uniform tube or a flat field. Fixing that meant notching it back with dark, not blending more. And dark dragged into wet light only dabbles, so the reflection had to set before I could darken it opaque.
