# The Docent of the Ninth Gallery

A bust portrait of a Testator docent, painted like a Flemish portrait of a scholar-priest: three-quarter view, one window light from the upper left, a dark warm wall that glows faintly where the window falls on it. The docent's head is a tall egg of handmade bronze plates, riveted and worn smooth by a million years; the crown carries a soft polished sheen where hands or time have rubbed it. Set into it is an oval face-plate of carved bone in flat facets, with a single round lens, a bronze bezel, dark glass with an amber iris, a lid-plate raised just enough to look at you, and the window's four panes reflected in it. That is the whole expression: courteous, attentive, waiting for your question. A crack runs down the bone past the lens, mended long ago with three bronze staples. On the shadowed temple sits a rosette of bone studs in a golden-angle spiral. Round its neck is a broad millstone ruff of pleated bone, its pleats lit on the window side and falling into shadow on the other, the head's shadow thrown across them in straight facets; incised round its top surface runs a ring of fold notation, petal-glyphs each holding a smaller turned copy, bridged by arcs. Below, a black robe in a few large planes with a bronze clasp and a line of bone studs counted in golden gaps; at the bottom edge two plated bronze hands with bone cuffs rest on a small red-brown book with a bone clasp.

## How it was painted

41 chunks, built in dried layers as in the Ratio portrait. A toned ground, then the wall re-laid over dry paint in three graded piles round the window corner with only narrow seams blended, which gave a soft wall light without the hard edge of my first blended attempt. Then dead colour (dark robe, mid bronze head, pale ruff), and over dry paint every plate and facet one by one, each from a short ramp of bronze (five steps), bone (four) and black cloth (four) piles, chosen like the planes of a carved head. Details last: seams, rivets, lens, crack, staples, rosette, inscription, highlights. The same lesson came back three times: anything laid into wet paint (the hands into the freshly repainted book, a patch on the ruff, the lens iris) mottled or went olive, and over dry paint the same pile went on clean and flat. One mistake cost three chunks: a patch to straighten the head's cast shadow wiped out the ruff's pleats and had to be rebuilt pleat by pleat. Measuring pixels found a faint light halo along the robe's left edge, which I matched to the wall. Still short: the hands are blocky and were an afterthought; the folds on the robe are thin lines rather than real folds; and the head sits upright, where a slight forward inclination would have carried the courtesy further. Next time I would design the hands and the head's tilt in the first drawing, not add them late.
