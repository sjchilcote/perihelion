# The Lesson

A dim bronze exhibit hall in the School. On the left, an arched bronze lesson plate is inlaid in bone with the fold notation for one room. A square room-glyph carries notches with chevrons on its far and near edges. A bridge arc leaves the far edge and comes back into the near edge: the room opens onto itself. Inside the glyph sit smaller copies of the room, each 0.618 of the last, and a red tick marks the visitor at the same place in every copy. Along the bottom of the plate runs the lesson sequence: a single glyph, two glyphs bridged, this self-gluing, and last a bridge that runs off into nothing, the mark the docents decline to translate. In the middle stands the docent. It is a tall Testator bell of bronze lamellae with a sunflower head of petals around a bone face, one petal missing, and a crack weeping verdigris. One thin arm points into the plate's inset; the other opens toward the great arch on the right. Through the arch is the small, warmly lit demonstration room the plate describes. The Commando's frame stands there in his red tabard with the bone cross, seen from behind. Through the doorway in the far wall he sees his own back, smaller, and beyond that a speck of himself again. The room's address is inlaid in its floor, and the join is carved over the arch before you walk through it. Two empty bronze chairs, with golden-spiral bone studs on their backs, wait in the foreground. It is a classroom with good chairs, and the notation, the space and the experience of it are in one picture.

How it was painted: the hall went in broadly in dark burnt sienna and bone black, the demonstration room in pale lead white and ochre planes. The nested doorways reuse one helper that draws the room's perspective at a smaller scale and offset. The fold notation was laid in pointed round strokes of bone mixture on the dry bronze plate, and it went down crisp and legible. The figures are drawn as flat silhouettes. What made the frame read was a thin warm rim where he meets the bright wall, and a cast shadow toward us. Mistakes: a rifle slung across his back cut through the cross on the tabard, so I painted the tabard out and restated it. A late repaint of the glow round the arch buried the first threshold carving, and I recarved it on a small bronze cartouche. Unclipped body strokes around the arch gave the glow a furry, stepped edge that I never fully smoothed. With more time I would model the docent's bell and the frame's limbs as rounded forms, not flat bands, and make the hall's glow a true gradient.

## Second pass

At the director's request I gave the figures form, cleaned up the light, and deepened the carving. Everything else is as before.

- **The glow around the arch** is repainted as five concentric bands, from warm ochre at the arch to the dark wall, with the strokes running along each band (the brush follows the arch's curve) and only the seams blended. The furry, stepped edge is gone.
- **The light on the floor** is now a clean pool. It spreads from the arch's sill in four graded zones, and its sides are softened with strokes laid along the edges.
- **The docent's bell** is rebuilt as nine separate bronze lamellae, lit from the arch. Each has a dark groove on one edge and a lit lip on the other, with a worn sheen down the lit side. It also has a bone hem band with studs, bone rings on a rounded spine, and lit rims on the petals that face the arch.
- **The Commando's frame** has lit flanks and a dark core on each limb, knee and elbow joints, warm light bouncing up from the floor onto the shins, lit tops on the shoulders and helmet, and a fine bright rim where he meets the bright room.
- **The plate** now has a sunken well holding the insets, which turn by the same angle with every lap of the loop and spiral down to a point. The visitor's red tick turns with them. Every carved line is recut with a shadow on its upper edge and a lit lower edge. The docent's finger is lengthened so its bone tip touches the corner of the turned inset.

What surprised me: the first recut of the turned insets came out as rounded blobs, because a brush stroke drawn through the corners of a square smooths it into a curve. I painted the whole plate field over again and recut every line one straight edge at a time.
