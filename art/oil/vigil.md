# The Long Vigil

The last evening of the watch. The Sun has stopped being a thing in the sky and
become the sky. Its disc rises behind the habitat ring like a sunset that will
never finish setting: white-gold where it meets the hull, deepening through
cadmium and vermilion to a blood-red limb, with a violet dark above it and no
stars left in the glare. Left of centre, small and seen from behind, the old
frame sits on the ring's scale-plate armour, one knee drawn up, a grey
outpost-made plate patched onto its back and the hazard paint worn off its
arm. Its hand rests beside a small lantern-like instrument with a warm window.
That is Ratio, and it is the only light in the picture that belongs to them.
The bronze plates run away to a horizon so hot it has turned to light. On the
right, the solar sail they built and never needed hangs from its mast in torn
tongues and threads against the fire. The two shadows fall toward us. Nobody is
leaving.

## How it was painted

I made it in 149 chunks on a warm tan ground at 1.6:1. The Sun went down first
as seventeen concentric bands of lead white, lemon chrome, cadmium, chrome,
Mars orange, vermilion and madder, laid on a radius that was pulled brighter
near the horizon behind the figure. The first surprise was the blending. Blends
run along the bands only left contour lines, but one blend running radially
across the whole wet disc dragged the bands into flame streaks that all lead to
the seated figure. I kept that, and later tied the disc together with about
2,600 short radial strokes of neighbouring band colours. The hull is graded
copper bands, from hot white-gold at the horizon down to bone-brown and black.
Over them I drew about 1,500 scale lips with a helper, each one a dark U-arc
with a thin lit line above it, shrinking toward the horizon. The paint fought
back several times. A glaze over the dark sky came out as smoky clouds. An
`edge="lost"` pass swallowed the hot rim. An unclipped blend dragged wet yellow
a hundred units down onto the hull. A distant outpost silhouette read as a
ship, and "no more ships" is the point of the picture, so I lifted it off wet.
Every attempt at a soft glow pool around the lamp failed: puddles, olive smears
or specks. Clean blender passes on wet paint saved most of these mistakes.
Next time I would plan the sail as torn film from the start, not as a curtain,
and I would build the lamp's halo before the hull's last layer went down, so
that it could be fused in wet.
