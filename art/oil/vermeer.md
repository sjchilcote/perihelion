# Docent Reading a Tablet at an Open Window

After Vermeer's "Girl Reading a Letter at an Open Window" and "Woman in Blue Reading a Letter". A Testator docent stands in profile at a leaded casement aboard the TESTAMENT, reading a small bone tablet of fold notation held up before its single lens. It is the warning, which it has read every day for a million years. The light is cool and pearly. It comes through the window on the left, falls across a wall of pale bronze that fades and cools away from the window, and throws the docent's soft shadow on the wall behind it. The docent has my design from "The Docent of the Ninth Gallery": a bowed egg of bronze plates; a bone face-plate in flat facets, seen edge-on; the lens a short barrel under a lowered lid, pointed down at the tablet; a millstone ruff of pleated bone with a ring of glyphs. It wears Vermeer's blue: a jacket lit along its window edge over a quietly pleated skirt. A bronze hand of jointed fingers grips the tablet's edge. In the foreground a table is heaped with a rumpled red carpet, with a blue band and a medallion of ochre, blue and rose lozenges set out along a golden-angle spiral. On it sits a tipped bronze dish of the orange-like fruit, two of them rolled loose. A green curtain hangs at the right edge. In one pane of the window, faintly, the bronze of the docent's head is reflected back.

## How it was painted

I painted it in 35 chunks, building in layers over dry paint. First a cool pale ground; then the wall graded in rings round the window; then dead colour; then the head facets, the face-plate, the lens, the ruff pleats, the window leading, the carpet, the fruit and the folds. My first wall came out warm cream, not Vermeer's pearl. Repainting it over dry paint with more ultramarine ash and a breath of zinc white gave the cool daylight. A later patch made a flat rectangle on the wall, and only re-laying the whole right side of the wall in graded vertical bands, blended at the seams while wet, made it seamless again. The fruit dish, laid into the wet carpet band, went translucent and had to be repainted opaque after drying, along with the fruit. The carpet pattern laid as touches came out as specks too small to read. Laid as masked lozenges along the spiral, it reads as a pattern. The lower robe took the most repairs. Old fold planes showed through and the smoothed outline made lumps, until I re-laid the skirt as one clean shape, with a jacket hem above it, and quieted its pleats. Still short of Vermeer: the light in the room is pale but not luminous; the window's glass lacks his glinting; the skirt is a stiff trapezoid; the carpet is flat rather than heaped; and the docent's head is more carved bronze than a figure absorbed in reading. Its bow is slight.
