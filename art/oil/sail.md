# The Sail

The Sun is enormous and close. Its disc fills the right of the frame, with a crisp limb and a corona of flame-streaks burning out into navy space. In front of it, on the TESTAMENT's sunward hull, four great masts rise past the top of the picture with a sail going up between them. The yards and rigging belong to a ship, but the canvas is a thin reflective membrane. The lower courses are set and billow toward the Sun. Each bay is dark where it hangs from the spars and has a gleam of reflected sunlight running down its belly, and the bays nearest the Sun glow gold like a cathedral window. Higher up the sail is only half unfurled: two bays are dropping partway, and the rest is still rolled along the upper yards. Fans of stays run from the masts down to anchors on the plated hull and catch the light as gold threads against the dark. Over the disc they show as black tracery. Drones crawl the yards, masts and membrane, small dark machines with amber eyes, and a few throw welding sparks. On the second yard, against the orange of the corona, stands the Commando's frame, a figure a few units tall, reaching up into the rigging. The sail's shadow falls across the plated hull toward us, and rows of lit windows show inside it. In front, the bronze plates of the hull run away to a curved horizon with hard backlit rims. Hope as engineering: a ship's rig, built on a bronze world, set against the thing that is pulling it down.

I painted the Sun as about 5,000 short strokes. Each one took its paint from a nine-step ramp chosen by its distance from the centre, laid around the disc inside it and out from it in the corona. A badger blend clipped to the corona turned the bands into flame-streaks, and one clipped to the disc smoothed it and left the limb crisp. The sail fought me. My first lay-in was a flat pale sheet, and a reflection pattern driven by noise turned it into camouflage blobs. What worked was to treat every bay as cloth billowing on its spars: dark at the edges, a vertical gleam where it bellies toward the Sun, and strokes laid along the gores. That pass buried the masts and stays inside the sail. I repainted the masts, and the old stays now read as rigging behind the membrane, which is a happy accident. Next time I would paint the sail before the masts and rigging and plan its billows from the start. I would also give the masts truss structure rather than plain poles, to make their size even plainer.
