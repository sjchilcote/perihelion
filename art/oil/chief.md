# The Chief of Miriad-7

A bust portrait of the grey-haired MIRIAD crew chief from the crew photograph, the man whose empty rooms the Commando walks through in the outpost. He stands against a dark brown wall, turned a little toward a cold strip light at the left edge of the frame. The light catches the far side of his face, his cropped grey hair and the brow ridge, and leaves the near cheek and jaw in warm shadow. He is middle-aged and tired: heavy lids over brown eyes, the lower lids lifted a touch, a few crow's feet on the lit side, grey brows, a closed mouth that settles a little at the corners, and a few days' grey stubble on the jaw and upper lip. He wears the navy MIRIAD work coverall with its collar open at the throat, a silver reflective band across the chest, and the amber hazard patch with its chevron on the shoulder nearest the light. He holds his clipboard flat against his chest, one hand curled over its edge in the light and the other gripping it in shadow, with a few lines of writing on the sheet. He was meant to read as a real person, painted with care.

## How it was painted

I painted it in 33 chunks over dry layers. The head is built from anatomical planes, each given its value by which way it faces the light: forehead, brow, almond eye sockets, the front and side planes of the nose, cheekbones, muzzle, chin and a squared jaw. A cool half-tone runs where the far side turns away. The features went on over dry paint as small masked shapes. My first pass of rectangular planes, blended at the seams, gave a mannequin with halos round every plane. Redrawn without blending, as shapes that follow the skull, they read as a carved head. The first eyes stared, with too much white, a purple, fully exposed iris and tick-mark brows. Lowering the upper lids over the iris, warming the irises and dimming the whites, especially on the shadow side, made them tired and kind. The first lips were too pink and full and the jaw too narrow, which read as young and ambiguous. Thin, muted lips, a squarer jaw and stubble made him a middle-aged man. The first stubble was a dense beard that spilled past the face; I repainted the muzzle and laid a much lighter one. The coverall began as a bright, rounded bell and became darker straight planes with a higher collar, and the background re-lay left hard steps until it was laid again as graded bands with their seams blended wet. Still short: the planes are visible rather than turned smoothly as a Flemish master would turn them; the hands are blocky; the head is small for the frame.
