# The Warden of the Ninth Niche

A Testator defender standing in its arched niche, as it has for a million years. It is tall and robed, built like a reliquary. Eleven long bronze lames hang from its belt as a skirt. A bone lancet set into the chest plate carries a column of its makers' script, and a golden-spiral nimbus is studded with bone. Its face is a sunflower: thirteen bronze petals closed around an oval of bone, which is set with seed-studs in a golden-angle spiral, and a single dark eye sits at the centre. In one hand it holds a leaf-bladed spear with a bone collar, planted like a staff of office. It raises the other palm-out, the old gesture that might mean *halt*, *peace* or *welcome*, and no longer reliably knows which. The light comes from high on the left. It throws the warden's shadow onto the back of the niche, and in that shadow the arm hangs at its side, as if it never rose. The corruption doesn't replace anything; it defaces. A band of the face has slipped sideways out of register, and the eye has moved with it and turned cold. One sector of the nimbus has broken out and floats displaced over the arch, lit from the wrong side. A second, cold arm with one finger too many reaches in a different pose. Straight cuts slice through it and run on into the moulding. A faint second contour shivers around the halo. One angular cold scrawl runs through the curving inscription, the only straight-edged writing on the whole figure. At its feet lies a fallen MIRIAD helmet, grey with an amber band, cracked across the visor. It shows what the warden has decided about strangers lately.

## How it was painted

The painting has 152 chunks over about 178 days of painting time. I used a dark warm toned ground, chalk drawing, then dark-to-light work on a wall of golden-spiral scale-plate lips. My first approach was to shade each part in value bands taken from a solid form. It came out posterized and wooden, like a mannequin. What worked was a small "tube" routine for every limb and lame: an opaque base, one lit filbert stroke, one dark core stroke, a hairline highlight and a cool reflected line, all laid wet into wet. That reads as polished metal. Glazes were the hardest lesson. Unblended, they dried as dark crackle veins. Blended inside a zone mask, they left hard zone edges. Over navy darks they veiled the paint lighter, not darker. Robe and mantle had to be repainted opaque with darker piles. Only late, over thoroughly dry paint, did one wide graded glaze on the outer wall work: it pushed the figure forward. Patching a light passage meant measuring the canvas's actual pixel colours and choosing a pile to match, because my eye kept picking piles a step too light. If I did it again, I would design the robe and the pauldrons properly before laying them in, keep the ghost arm duller from the start (its first version was a blue rubber glove), and give the light more drama on the figure earlier, rather than adding highlights at the end.
