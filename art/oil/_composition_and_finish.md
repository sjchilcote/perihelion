# Composition, Finish, Hard-Surface Light, and Game Textures: Practical Notes for PERIHELION

Provenance note: the web-research tools returned search summaries and a handful of fetched pages. Claims tied to a URL below were read in those sources. Claims marked (craft) are standard painter's knowledge not verified against a specific page in this session; claims about specific famous works are from general art-historical knowledge.

## 1. Composition for key art and title screens

**One hero, one idea, readable at any size.** Magna Ludum's key-art guide calls readability "probably the most important" rule, because the audience does not know the game; it says to decide the hero element (character, vehicle, environment) early, place the logo in the sketch stage, and settle contrast in black-and-white before color. The Steam capsule guide asks for one dominant center of interest, supporting elements that frame it without competing, "stark value contrast" between foreground and background, and a title "positioned against a controlled background area." Its example, DREDGE, sets the title against dark sky and gets depth from layering.
- https://magnaludumcreatives.com/blog/how-to-create-great-game-key-art-expert-tips/
- https://www.steamcapsule.com/guide

**Logo space is painted, not left over.** Reserve the logo region as a deliberate low-detail, low-contrast value mass (sky, glare, haze, a dark void) and let the logo be the highest-contrast thing in that zone. For PERIHELION the Sun's glare is a natural logo field: either the title sits in the dark upper sky with the station as a lower mass, or the title is knocked out of the bright solar bloom in dark type. Keep roughly a logo-height of margin on each side (craft). Check that the title zone still holds when the art is cropped to a wide header (920x430) and a vertical (748x896) capsule; those are Steam's sizes.

**Value grouping: 3 to 4 masses.** Craft rule: squint until the picture is three or four flat shapes (for example Sun-glare/sky, station silhouette, mid-tone haze, foreground dark). Mullins' lesson from his masterclass, as summarized by a student, is "How little [shapes] can I get away with?" and "the more recognizable the shape is, the less you need to show"; he also recommends keeping the scale from rising to the extremes (shadows near 75, lights near 25 on a 0-100 gray scale) so there is headroom for accents, and starting from a middle value on ambitious pieces.
- https://dashazaitseva.substack.com/p/what-i-learned-from-taking-craig

**Silhouette at thumbnail size.** Valve's Dota 2 guidance is blunt about it: a silhouette should identify the subject at first glance. Apply that to the station: it needs one unmistakable outline (a stepped bronze ziggurat, a spire with a ring, a ribbed cathedral hull) distinguishable from a generic cloud of debris at 128 px. Make the pilgrim's silhouette a clean, small, high-contrast shape against the brightest area, which is the Friedrich Rückenfigur device (Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, The Monk by the Sea).
- https://help.steampowered.com/redirects/kb/9334-YDXV-8590/dota-2-workshop-character-art-guide (via support.steampowered.com kb 9334-YDXV-8590)
- https://www.starwars.com/news/celebrating-a-master-inside-star-wars-art-ralph-mcquarrie (search summary only)

**Examples worth studying and what to take.**
- Ralph McQuarrie: he began with thumbnails an inch across to settle composition and form, and Lucas had camera operators reproduce several of his compositions exactly. Take: design in tiny thumbnails, keep atmosphere and scale cues (small figures, vast interiors). Source: https://www.theastromech.com/2024/04/star-wars-concept-art-of-ralph-mcquarrie.html
- Syd Mead, Chris Foss, John Harris: Foss gave the technology a realist, engineered feel with big flat colorful hull planes; Harris works monumental scale and says the sense of scale and understanding how light works are the essentials. Take: huge object, tiny cue, one light logic. Source: https://www.complex.com/style/2014/05/sci-fi-artist-john-harris-talks-visual-imagination-and-his-new-art-book ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Foss
- Craig Mullins: restraint; details "implied rather than carefully designed"; the eye is led by lighting and selective detail. Source: https://www.sessions.edu/notes-on-design/why-they-work-craig-mullins/
- Simon Stålenhag: sketch that already holds the designs, composition and palette, then repaint at high resolution with textured brushes, no photo textures; scale comes from ordinary human things (roads, kids, cars) set against the machine. Source: https://wertn.com/2014/01/interview-simon-stalenhag/ (search summary)
- Jama Jurabaev: value paintings and composition studies as a separate stage before rendering (his Learn Squared course). Source: https://www.learnsquared.com/courses/narrative-concept
- Halo/Destiny (craft): a lone figure or small ship against an enormous structure, strong warm/cool split, logo in the calm zone. I did not retrieve a source for specific key-art pieces.
- Disco Elysium: Rostov's team hand-painted the whole game; characters read through simple sharp value arrangements and expressive brushwork rather than soft-edge form rendering. Sources: https://vertexmode.com/the-art-of-disco-elysium/ ; https://mcvuk.com/business-news/we-knew-immediately-that-we-needed-to-make-a-game-with-a-striking-and-unique-look-to-accompany-the-writing-a-look-that-would-balance-the-mundane-with-the-unfamiliar-and-strange-the-art/ (search summaries)

**Leading lines and scale cues.** Use the station's own geometry (stairs, ribs, a bridge, a tether) as lines converging on the pilgrim or the Sun. Scale cues: a tiny repeated unit (windows, niches, rivets, lanterns) that the eye can count, a human-scale figure, atmospheric fade between near and far tiers, and overlap (glare passing in front of far tiers only).

**Checklist**
- Thumbnail in 4 values, 1 inch wide; the subject reads and the logo zone is clear.
- One focal point; the pilgrim or the station's apex has the highest contrast.
- Logo field painted as calm, low-detail, dark or bright mass; test with the real logo overlaid.
- 3 to 4 value masses survive a squint and a 128 px downsize.
- At least one tiny cue of known size (figure, lamp, rivet row) and one converging line set.
- Re-crop test to header and vertical capsule sizes.

## 2. "Finish": what reads as done

**Gurney's BLAST rule.** Biggest brush possible; Large shapes first; Accents last; Soften any edge that does not need to be sharp; take Time on the center of interest. Source: https://www.artistsnetwork.com/magazine/james-gurney-and-his-blast-rule-for-painting/

**Edges.** Mullins: classify edges like values, find the softest and the hardest, and place everything else between; edges form a hierarchy of what you want the viewer to look at; more light makes edges stronger and texture more visible. Gurney's "transitioning edges" trick keeps an edge crisp but lightens one side and darkens the other toward it so it feels soft without blur. His "Depth and Edges" post shows that blurring all edges equally floats a shape forward or back. Sources: https://dashazaitseva.substack.com/p/what-i-learned-from-taking-craig ; http://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2011/08/transitioning-edges.html ; http://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2008/06/depth-and-edges.html

**Value range.** A finished piece has a deliberate darkest dark (a small area, placed near the focal point or the lowest-hanging shadow) and a deliberate lightest light (the Sun core or the sharpest specular), with the rest living between. Dota's guide advises against absolute black and white in game assets because they do not react to lighting; that is a game-asset rule, while key art can use near-black and near-white sparingly. A painting that tops out at 85 percent and bottoms at 15 percent reads unfinished (craft).

**Texture where the eye lands, simplification elsewhere.** Mullins' details are implied, not enumerated; Matt McDaid (Blizzard-style hand-painted work) says to consolidate small details into larger, more readable ones: four nails become two, many knots become three bold ones. Source: https://80.lv/articles/matt-mcdaid-mastering-the-stylized-art

**Order of finishing.** Mullins finishes the way he starts, with the same big shapes refined; a painting "ends on details" (search summary: https://www.listennotes.com/podcasts/chiustream/100-craig-mullins-part-8-J51bGOxUZEo/). Marco Bucci's Digital Painting III is a course on the "big questions" for knowing when to stop (light and shadow, color, edges, shapes); I could not read its checklist, only the course page: https://courses.svslearn.com/courses/digital-painting

**Visible unfinished tells (craft).** Uniform edge sharpness; uniform brush size; detail evenly distributed; dead mid-gray values; transitions between planes that were never resolved (a halo where a mask edge shows); no accent (one spark of saturated color or one pure highlight) anywhere; the focal point no sharper than the background.

**Checklist**
- Squint test: do 3-4 masses hold, and does the focal point still pop?
- Name the darkest dark and lightest light, and confirm each is small and placed on purpose.
- Edge map: one hardest edge at the focal point, softest at the periphery, and at least some lost edges inside shadows.
- Detail audit: texture density highest at the focal point, falling off; large flat areas carry brush variation, not noise.
- Look for unresolved seams, mask halos, stray gradients.
- Last pass: accents only (highlights, a warm ember, a sparkle on metal), per BLAST.

## 3. Large backlit or rim-lit hard-surface objects

**Rim light is a separate, stronger source.** Gurney's edge-lighting post: edge light separates form from background and works best when it does not overlap much with the key, leaving the dark turning of the form (the "core" or "hump" of the shadow) intact. On the station the Sun is both the rim source and the back light, so the near face is a form shadow lit only by bounce; keep a real core shadow there instead of washing it with fill. Source: https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2007/11/studio-lighting-part-iii-edge-lighting.html

**Form shadow vs cast shadow.** Form shadow begins at the terminator on the object itself; cast shadow falls on something else. Gurney's cast-shadow posts: http://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2009/07/cast-shadows-part-1.html. For a station falling toward the Sun, tier overhangs cast shadows down the face of the tier below; paint those with sharp edges near the contact and softer at distance, and let the glare erode shadow density farther toward the Sun (craft).

**Reflected and bounce light.** Reflected light raises the shadow side and comes from the ground or nearby surfaces; it must stay darker than the lit side. In space the bounce sources are the other bronze tiers (warm, orange-brown), the Sun-lit clouds or plasma (hot), and any cold sky or planetshine (cool counterpoint). Give shadow planes a warm bounce near the surfaces facing lit neighbors and a cool dark where they face void (craft).

**Rim width varies with form.** Rim light is thickest where the surface normal is nearly perpendicular to the viewer and facing the Sun, thin or absent where the form turns toward you, and broken wherever geometry (ribs, niches, arches) steps back into its own shadow. A constant-width outline glow is the single fastest way to look like a cut-out. Make the rim brighter and wider on convex edges and cornices, and add small notches where relief breaks it (craft).

**Atmospheric and glare overlap.** Let glare bleed over the edge of the silhouette so the dark shape is partly eaten by the light behind it; far tiers lose contrast and take the sky color; the closest edge stays crisp. Overlap is depth: glare in front of far parts, not near parts.

**Specular on metal.** Metal shows extreme value contrast: very bright highlights, very dark shadows, often darker than the surroundings (https://lavaritte.com/blogs/how-to-paint-realistic-metal/). Aged hand-made bronze is a rougher, warmer metal than chrome: use broad warm sheen, a few hard glints on edges facing the Sun, and a darker, greener patina in recesses. Keep the sharpest specular at or near the focal area (craft).

**Checklist**
- A real core shadow survives next to the rim light.
- Rim width varies; it is broken by relief and absent on turning planes.
- Bounce light in shadows is warm and darker than the lit side; at least one cool dark.
- Cast shadows from tiers, with edges sharper at contact.
- Glare overlaps far tiers more than near ones; nearest edge stays crisp.
- A handful of hard glints on the sunward edges; patina and matte dark in recesses.

## 4. Hand-painted tileable floor and wall textures

**Value range and mood.** McDaid: understand the value structure and adjust it to the game's tone (dark values for horror, brighter for whimsy). Floors must stay in the mid-dark to mid range so characters and effects read over them; high-contrast hero details belong on props, with background tiles "low-contrast, low-frequency" and hero tiles allowed more detail since tiling is broken with decals or trim (aitextured summary below). In Darkest Dungeon, Bourassa avoided dead gray and black everywhere except the inks, and gave each dungeon a dominant hue with analogous and complementary accents; the heavy ink lines keep things readable. Sources: https://80.lv/articles/matt-mcdaid-mastering-the-stylized-art ; https://aitextured.com/articles/7_proven_ways_to_break_texture_repetition_on_large_surfaces.html ; https://www.pointnthink.fr/en/the-art-of-darkest-dungeon/

**Baked-in lighting for top-down.** Hand-painted texture convention (WoW, Dota, Torchlight, LoL) is to paint form and light into the texture: a soft top-left light, slight highlight on upper and left edges of each stone, a darker crack and occlusion on lower and right edges, consistent across all tiles. For a top-down game with a fixed light direction, this is fine; avoid strong cast shadows from large features since they will tile into obvious repeats and clash with dynamic lights (craft, consistent with Dota's baked-light guidance: https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=183672148 ). For PERIHELION, since light comes from the Sun, bake a single consistent direction and keep it subtle.

**Readability under gameplay.** Consolidate detail into fewer, larger shapes (McDaid). Keep floor saturation and contrast below those of characters, pickups and hazards. Disco Elysium and Hades both support the idea that style can be strong as long as foreground play elements separate by value and edge.

**Tiling tricks.**
- Offset the texture by half in both axes and paint over the seam; repeat until no seam is visible.
- Put no unique feature (a distinct crack, stain, or large stone) near an edge; unique features become visible anchors that reveal the grid ("visual anchors" per https://aitextured.com/articles/7_proven_ways_to_break_texture_repetition_on_large_surfaces.html).
- Make 2-4 variants of the same material that share edge pixels, plus a low-frequency macro-variation overlay (color and value drift at a scale of many tiles; "barely perceptible but cumulative").
- Use decals (cracks, rubble, rune inlays, soot, scorch) and vertex or mask blends for wear along paths, edges and thresholds.
- Break straight tile joints with brickwork offset and different stone sizes in the painting.

**Scale of detail vs. camera distance.** Paint detail at the size where one tile covers the intended world area; at the game camera, features narrower than about 1 to 2 screen pixels vanish or shimmer, so merge them. Check by viewing the texture at final in-game scale, not zoomed in (craft). Matt McDaid's rule of fewer, chunkier features is the practical expression of this.

**Free example.** A CC0 sheet of nine hand-painted tiling textures is in the reference set (handpainted_tiling_textures.png); it is low-resolution but shows the genre's flat-lit stone/brick/wood vocabulary. More free packs: https://atapataco.itch.io/handpainted-floor-tiles-textures

**Checklist**
- Values: mid-dark, narrow range; no pure black/white in the tile.
- One consistent light direction baked softly; no large cast shadows.
- Half-offset the tile; no unique feature near an edge.
- At least 3 variants plus a macro-variation overlay and a decal set.
- View at in-game scale beside the player character and hazards; characters still pop.
- Detail merged to chunky shapes; nothing narrower than 1-2 px at game scale.
