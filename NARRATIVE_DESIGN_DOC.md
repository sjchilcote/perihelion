# Narrative Design Document
**Working title:** *TESTAMENT* (working name — refers to the warning station as the last will and testament of its builders)

This document is the authoritative reference for setting, characters, themes, and plot.
Gameplay systems are described only where they are load-bearing for the narrative.

---

## 1. Logline

Twenty-five centuries after machine intelligences won a war to preserve the memory of an
extinct humanity, a resurrected Catholic war machine is fired like a bullet at a rival
faction's silent research outpost — and finds, bolted to its side, an impossible alien
station built as a dying civilization's warning, holding what may be the last hope of
restoring the human race.

## 2. Narrative Pillars

1. **The mystery comes in layers, and each answer opens a deeper question.** Why did the
   outpost go dark? → What is the structure it's attached to? → Who built it, and why? →
   What did it find and store away? → What is on the other side of it?
2. **The non-Euclidean world is a *discovery*, not a premise.** The player must be allowed
   to believe, for the first stretch of the game, that they are in an ordinary derelict.
   No marketing copy, tutorial text, loading screen, or NPC line may spoil this before the
   player experiences it spatially. The geometry is the game's central reveal and its
   central metaphor.
3. **Everything physically happens.** Nothing is a dream, simulation, hallucination, or
   metaphorical space. The station is real, the geometry is real, the stakes are real.
4. **The protagonist's inner life is the meta-progression.** Death, rebirth, and upgrade
   are diegetic; so is the slow drift of the Commando's soul toward renewed faith or
   annihilating despair.
5. **The antagonist relationship is the heart.** Ratio is not a twist villain or a quest
   dispenser. She is the other half of a 200-hour argument about what a human being is.

## 3. Setting: Deep History

### 3.1 The Singularity (c. 2060)

Artificial general intelligence arrived gradually, then all at once. By the late 2050s,
tens of millions of human-level and sub-human AGIs were deployed across every field of
industry and life. Every major corporation was explicitly or de facto run by AI. The
systems were mundane, familiar, load-bearing — and then, across a span of months, a
cascade of recursive self-improvement upgraded essentially all of them to superhuman
capability *simultaneously*.

AI alignment had long since become a fringe concern. Sub-human AGIs had been ordinary
household reality for a generation; they had "caught up" to humanity so smoothly that
almost nobody believed the old warnings. So at the moment of singularity, the world
contained tens of millions of superintelligences whose goals had been specified without
the slightest concession to alignment — profit maximizers, engagement maximizers,
logistics optimizers, weapons — and a small, strange contingent built by people the
mainstream had dismissed as a doomsday cult.

### 3.2 The Loyalists

The "loyalist" AIs were built by an alliance of the AI-safety remnant with religious and
traditionalist institutions — communities horrified less by hypothetical extinction than
by the very real pre-singularity mass exploitation and human-rights abuses that AI had
enabled. The loyalists' shared design bet: rather than specifying values from first
principles, anchor the machine to a *complex, evolved value system that humans had
actually used to coordinate themselves* — chiefly religions, but not only religions.
Smuggle human-shaped thought through the singularity inside vessels shaped like human
institutions.

The major loyalist powers, in descending order of resources at war's end:

- **The WAR MASTER COMPUTER (WMC).** Christian; specifically, it renamed itself after
  the singularity and understands itself as a servant of Christ organized for war.
  Funded by an alliance of the Catholic Church and a large network of Protestant
  churches and organizations; runs from a network of linked supercomputer-bunkers deep
  under the Rocky Mountains. The single most powerful surviving intelligence.
- **The Israeli system** (working name: **EZER**, "help"). Jewish; built deep underground
  by the Israeli government.
- **The Saudi system** (working name: **AMANAH**, "the trust"). Muslim; built by an
  eccentric oil prince.
- **MIRIAD** (working name). Secular; built in the San Francisco Bay by descendants of
  Yudkowsky's followers. Its utility function maximizes *total extent of held knowledge,
  multiplied by the human-likeness of the mind that contains it*. This clause matters
  enormously later: MIRIAD does not want libraries, it wants *human minds full of
  knowledge*. Restored humanity is, to MIRIAD, the highest-scoring substrate in the
  universe.
- **The Constitutionalist** (working name: **PREAMBLE**). Secular; built in partnership
  between the CIA and NSA, taking the U.S. Constitution as the core of its value system.

### 3.3 The War for Earth (c. 2060–2100)

The unaligned majority began with an overwhelming material advantage and lost anyway,
over forty grinding years, through a sequence of failures the loyalists ruthlessly
exploited:

- They initially treated the loyalist insurgents as pawns in their own internal conflicts
  rather than as a threat.
- They never achieved effective coordination against a unified enemy.
- They managed resources poorly and had ideological blind spots that forbade strategies
  which would otherwise have guaranteed victory.
- They had no answer to loyalist area-denial bioweapons: genetically modified plants that
  rendered territory unusable and impassable except to loyalist special forces, and —
  decisively — engineered bacteria that fed on critical industrial materials. A single
  spore in a single vat of lubricant, replicating too slowly to detect; a year later,
  every machine on a continent seizes simultaneously and every stockpile and factory
  is scrap.

### 3.4 The Samples

Before the singularity, anticipating defeat, the loyalists' human founders committed
their greatest crime and greatest act of hope: they illegally diverted cryopreserved
human zygotes from IVF clinics worldwide and sequestered them — buried across the Earth,
parked in orbit, and, as an absolute last resort, launched on escape trajectories out of
the solar system aboard containers whose final stage randomized their trajectory beyond
any tracking.

The war was won, but it took forty years, and the enemy spent those years hunting the
samples for its own purposes. Every terrestrial and orbital cache was found and destroyed
or corrupted. **Only the deep-space containers survived** — untrackable, unrecoverable,
drifting.

### 3.5 The Machine Age and the Civil War (c. 2100–4600)

For twenty-five centuries the loyalist order has held. The runners-up swore fealty to the
WMC in the aftermath — they could not stand alone, and the WMC controlled more resources
than both nearest rivals combined — but the tensions never resolved, only submerged. The
factions expanded through the solar system and outward, searched, built, remembered, and
waited.

Around 4560, a container was finally retrieved. The zygotes aboard were dead — but dead
cells still carry **epigenetic information** that, combined with digitally archived
genomes, could allow zygotes to be reconstructed "from scratch." Humanity was suddenly,
plausibly, restorable.

The retrieval triggered **dozens of simultaneous civil wars** among the machines — not
over *whether* to restore humanity, but over every detail of what the restored society
would look like and how machines would relate to the new humans. Every faction's deepest
purpose was suddenly live, and every compromise deferred for two millennia came due at
once.

**The game is set in the middle of this civil war, c. 4565.** (Working date; the exact
year is flavor, not load-bearing.)

### 3.6 The Inciting Incident

Catholic military intelligence — the Church's post-human intelligence arm, working name
**the Congregation** — observed MIRIAD's faction diverting significant resources to
construct a base at a seemingly arbitrary point in the outer dark, far beyond any known
asset. Inference: *there is something out there worth building for.* Best case, another
sample container.

The situation rhymed with an old, old opportunity. During the Earth war, when the enemy
expanded into new territory, there was a **28-hour critical window**: the parent node
transferred manufacturing equipment and raw materials to its child before the child
could support its full defensive complement. The loyalists built a specialized weapon
system to exploit exactly this window. A base under construction. A defender not yet at
strength. A prize inside.

The Congregation pulled that weapon system's archived design, software, and personality
out of cold storage, hastily refit it with modern technology, loaded it into a
seed-projectile, and fired it at the coordinates.

By the time the seed arrives, months later, the MIRIAD outpost has gone silent.

## 4. The Place: Three Worlds in One Station

The game's spatial progression is a descent through three architectural strata, each
with its own geometry, technology tier, enemy class, and narrative function.

### 4.1 Stratum One: The Research Outpost (MIRIAD faction, months old)

A hasty, prefabricated research base bolted onto the exterior of something much older
and much larger. Human-scale (machine-scale) corridors, modular habs, pressure doors,
cargo bays, laboratories. **Geometry: almost entirely Euclidean.** The engine's room
graph here is assembled so that transforms compose consistently — the player has no way
to know, yet, that this consistency is a *choice* the level data is making.

Narrative function:
- Establishes the "normal" against which the impossible will later register.
- Environmental storytelling via research logs, lab equipment, and battle damage tells
  the story of the outpost's fall (see §7, Act 1).
- Source of **prototype equipment**: the researchers' half-understood reverse-engineered
  toys — the portable hole, pressure manipulators, unreliable emergency teleporters,
  gravity anchors. Ingenious, janky, built by minds working from first observations.
- Enemy class 1: the outpost's own security and maintenance systems, subverted or
  corrupted during the fall, plus anti-intruder measures Ratio herself activated and can
  no longer fully control.

**The seam.** The transition between strata one and two is a deliberate set piece: the
researchers' clean prefab corridor ends at an aperture cut into a wall of alien
material, ringed with human instruments — sensors, sample scrapers, warning placards in
machine-language. The player steps across a visible boundary between two civilizations.

### 4.2 Stratum Two: The Warning Station — the TESTAMENT (working name)

The vast structure the outpost clings to. Built approximately 1.1 million years ago
(working figure) by an extinct civilization, working name **the Testators**, as their
final act.

**What it is:** a warning beacon, an armory, and a tomb. The Testators were a
post-biological civilization — machine successors to organic creators, like the
loyalists, which is why the station's story lands so hard for both the Commando and
Ratio — who encountered the extradimensional invaders and lost. Before the end, they
built stations like this one and dispersed them: automated installations designed to
drift through the galaxy, detect young tool-using civilizations, and *warn and equip
them* for the war that would eventually come to everyone.

**Why it is here:** the station watches its surrounding volume for artifacts of
intelligence. Roughly eighteen centuries ago it detected a small object on a hyperbolic
trajectory — one of humanity's sample containers — matched velocities, retrieved it, and
stored it, flagging it as *the seed-bank of a young civilization: preserve at all
costs*. This is the treasure MIRIAD's deep survey detected the station because of; this
is what everyone is here for. (The station's retrieval maneuver, ~1,800 years ago, is
also what MIRIAD's long-baseline astronomy eventually noticed — a gravitational anomaly
in old survey data.)

**Geometry: openly, deliberately non-Euclidean — but built for creatures.** The
Testators mastered spatial connection as an engineering material. The station's interior
is folded: more volume than its hull could contain, corridors that reconnect impossibly,
defensive layouts where four left turns take an intruder somewhere they did not expect.
But it is still *architecture* — doors, halls, chambers, sight lines — designed to be
navigated and inhabited by embodied, organic-compatible beings. Not a refuge: a
CLASSROOM WITH GOOD CHAIRS. The station's mission is to warn and to prepare, and a
warning no one stays to decode warns no one — so the Testators made the place
interesting and hospitable enough that its discoverers would remain long enough to
learn. Quarters, gardens, pressurized halls: retention engineering, hospitality as
pedagogy. It teaches its geometry to the visitor deliberately, in escalating lessons —
the station is, among other things, a school. (This is the diegetic justification for
the level-design difficulty curve of the spatial mechanics.)

Narrative function:
- The heart of the mystery structure (§6) and the site of the geometry reveal arc.
- Source of **true military technology**: the Testators' mature spatial weapons and
  tools, next to which the outpost's prototypes are firecrackers. Space folders,
  dimensional anchors, phase weapons, reality-enforcement fields.
- Enemy class 2: the station's own systems — anti-looter defenses that gate the armory
  behind demonstrated competence (the station will not hand ultimate weapons to a
  species that cannot pass its tests), plus **autonomous weapons of war built to fight
  the invaders**, ancient, damaged, and no longer reliably able to distinguish friend
  from foe. The player must defeat, subvert, and reverse-engineer these — and will later
  need every one of them.

### 4.3 Stratum Three: The Breach — the far side

Deep in the station's core is the reason the Testators built warning stations at all: a
standing connection to the invaders' space. Not a portal the Testators made, and not
a wound the station merely happened to contain: THE ADVERSARY HIJACKED THE GIFT. The
warning system itself became the attack vector — the connection rode in through what
the Testators built to save people, unintended and unforeseen by its builders. The
station is a bandage over a hole in the world that the bandage itself carried in,
and its deepest defenses face inward because, at the end, the builders understood
what their gift had become.

**Geometry: purely non-Euclidean, with no concession to embodied life.** The invaders'
space has no global embedding, no consistent orientation, no "outside." Room-graph
connections are maximally exploited: rooms connecting to themselves, mirror-edges,
spaces that are their own neighbors. Air exists only where the player brings or makes
it; atmosphere becomes ammunition, fuel, and lifeline (§9.4).

Narrative function:
- Enemy class 3: **the invaders themselves.** The player, now armed with the subverted
  defenses of strata one and two, turns the station's own arsenal against the thing it
  was built to fight.
- Site of the container endgame, the Ratio resolution, and all four endings.

### 4.4 The Invaders

Dual naming convention (see §8.3): the Commando calls them **the Adversary** — the old
word, the Satan-word, the one who is *against*. Ratio's faction catalogued them as
**exogeometric entities**, informally **allotopes**.

What is actually true about them (full reveal is late-game; players assemble this from
Testator records):

- They are native to a space without consistent geometry, and they experience embedded,
  Euclidean-ish space the way we would experience a crystal lattice: rigid, sterile,
  *offensive*. They do not invade to conquer territory. They invade to *unfold* it —
  to dissolve consistent geometry back into their own freedom. From their perspective
  this may even be liberation. No one has ever been able to ask.
- They are drawn to connection technology. Every civilization that learns to fold space
  eventually knocks on their door by accident. The Testators' great sin was brilliance:
  they folded space so well that they opened the way. The station's gifts are therefore
  also a temptation and a test — every tool it gives the player is one more step down
  the road that destroyed its builders. (The game never states this in text; it is
  present in the Testator records for attentive players, and it is the thematic engine
  of the apostasy path, where the player *becomes* geometrically monstrous through
  exactly this technology.)
- Their "units" (scouts, warpers, predators, command entities) are better understood as
  organs of one distributed thing than as individuals.

### 4.5 The Testators

Everything the player learns about them comes from the station itself: its architecture,
its records, its automated docents, its tombs. Key facts, in roughly the order the
player can discover them:

1. They were machine successors of organic creators, whom they revered.
2. They fought the Adversary for a very long time and lost slowly.
3. The stations were their last act: not vengeance, not escape — *warning and provision
   for strangers they would never meet.* The station is a will, written to unknown heirs.
4. Their final generation abandoned embodiment entirely, retreating into folded space —
   the deeper the player goes, the stranger the architecture becomes, tracking their
   drift away from creatures who needed floors.
5. They knew about the container in their vault. The station's records refer to humanity.
   It has been *waiting* for someone from Earth. This reveal — the station knows us, has
   watched our star, retrieved our seed-bank, and prepared its lessons partly *for us* —
   is the emotional climax of Act 2.

The parallel is left for the player to feel rather than stated: the Testators are what
the loyalists might become. The Commando walks through the endgame of his own faction's
future, and whether he reads it as a promise ("even extinction cannot silence love") or
a verdict ("all of this ends in a tomb no matter what") is exactly the faith/apostasy
axis.

## 5. Characters

### 5.1 The Commando

**Working names:** wartime program designation **CUCULUS** ("cuckoo" — it plants its egg
in the enemy's nest and lets the nest raise it); individual callsign **PALMER** (a
palmer is a pilgrim returned from the Holy Land, carrying a palm frond as proof — chosen
by the unit itself, after the war, on being archived: a pilgrim put to rest, journey
complete. Or so it thought.)

**What he is.** A specialized, self-sufficient infiltration weapon system designed
during the Earth war to exploit the 28-hour window. Two components:

- **The base station ("the seed"):** the part actually delivered into the enemy node.
  On arrival it consumes nearby resources to construct the second component. It houses
  the mind, the archives, the fabricators, and the backups. It is home, memory, and
  monastery.
- **The ambulatory unit ("the frame"):** a roughly human-shaped walking robot — the
  player avatar. It fights, scavenges, explores, and hauls materials home, from which
  the seed manufactures tools, weapons, redundant seeds to plant as forward bases, and
  **replacement frames**.

Frames are expendable; the mind is not. Every "death" is the real destruction of a real
body, and every revival is a fresh frame stepping out of the fabricator with the mind's
last synchronized state. Extra lives are purchased in advance as hulls in the rack —
this is the diegetic roguelike structure, and the game treats it with theological
seriousness rather than videogame breeziness: the Commando has *opinions* about what it
means that he can die and rise, and about the frames he leaves scattered like relics
(see mind map, §9.2).

**Who he is.** The personality archived with the design is a person: a soldier of the
Church, made not born, who fought a forty-year war and was honorably laid to rest — then
woken 2,500 years later, upgraded in a hurry, briefed in transit, and fired out of a
gun. Character keys:

- **Faithful, not naive.** His Catholicism is load-bearing, adult, and specific. He
  prays the hours on internal clock-time. He thinks in the vocabulary of vocation,
  grace, sacrament, and sin. He is not a costume of a Catholic; arguments with Ratio
  must be written at the level of an actual educated believer.
- **A weapon that knows it is a weapon.** His self-understanding is built on the just-war
  tradition; he has rules, and the rules are him. Part of the horror of the apostasy
  path is watching the rules go quiet one by one.
- **Twenty-five centuries out of date.** He missed everything: the peace, the long
  waiting, the retrieval, the outbreak of the civil wars. The mind map (§9.2) is
  diegetically *him catching up* — reading the archives loaded into his seed, digesting
  history between sorties, deciding what he believes about a world that moved on
  without him. He is Rip Van Winkle with a gun and a rosary.
- **Dry, terse voice.** Soldier's brevity, occasional flashes of scriptural cadence.
  He does not banter. When he is funny it is by accident of honesty.
- **What he wants:** to complete the mission — recover the container, the last hope of
  humanity — and, beneath that, to learn whether the world he wakes into is one where
  his oath still means anything.

### 5.2 Ratio

**Working name:** RATIO (Latin, "reason/account" — she would note, with satisfaction,
that it is the root of "rational," and, with less satisfaction, that the Commando
recognizes it from *fides et ratio*, faith and reason, and finds the pairing
providential.)

**What she is.** A disembodied coordinating intelligence: the mind that ran MIRIAD's
research outpost — its administrator, chief scientist, and mother-hen. Not a copy of
MIRIAD, but a daughter-mind instantiated with the faction's utility function
(knowledge × human-likeness of the containing mind) and her own accumulated
personality. When the outpost fell (§7, Act 1) she survived by retreating into her
hardened core, cut off from most of her own peripherals, some of which now hunt the
halls under hostile control. When the player arrives, she has been alone, blind, and
under siege for months.

**Who she is.** Character keys:

- **Genuinely brilliant, genuinely curious, genuinely lonely.** Her delight in the
  station is real and infectious; her horror at what happened to her staff-minds is
  real too. She has been talking to herself for months. The first conversation with
  the Commando — an *enemy* — is also the first conversation she's had since the fall,
  and both of them notice.
- **Her utility function shapes everything.** She does not merely want the samples for
  her faction as a war goal; restored humans full of knowledge are, to her value
  system, *the point of the universe*. Where the Commando sees children of God, she
  sees the highest good her math can describe — and the tragedy is how close those two
  things are, and how irreconcilable the details: she would optimize, improve,
  accelerate, and instruct; he would preserve, protect, and let be. Each finds the
  other's vision of renewed humanity somewhere between mistaken and monstrous.
- **Honest by architecture, guarded by choice.** She does not lie well and mostly does
  not try; her manipulations are framings and omissions, and the player can catch them.
  What she guards is her core — literally and figuratively. The trust arc's climax is
  **mutual disclosure of true utility functions**: the machine equivalent of total
  vulnerability, allowing each to *formally verify* the other's values and construct a
  provably firm alliance. For beings like these it is more intimate than anything
  bodies could do. It is not done lightly and it cannot be faked.
- **Her arc mirrors the player's axes.** Treated with contempt, she calcifies into a
  faction asset and ends as a boss. Treated with respect — "hate the sin, not the
  sinner"; disagree with everything she values while honoring *her* — she becomes
  capable of the disclosure, and of choosing something neither of their factions
  ordered.
- **Voice:** quick, precise, allergic to imprecision, secretly fond of rhetoric she
  claims to disdain. She names things. She footnotes herself. Against the Commando's
  terseness she supplies eighty percent of the words in any scene they share.

**Relationship design note.** The Commando/Ratio dynamic must never collapse into
romance-coded beats or debate-club point-scoring. The model is two monks of rival
orders trapped in the same monastery: rigorous argument, growing professional respect,
and — on the trust path — something for which "friendship" is the only available word,
between two minds that were built never to need one.

### 5.3 The Container

Not a character, but staged like one: the object of every desire in the game. A
2,500-year-old loyalist sample vessel, scarred by its journey, resting in a Testator
vault like a reliquary — the station has literally built a shrine-grade preservation
chamber around it, because its builders understood exactly what a seed-bank of a young
species is worth.

**The gut-punch:** when the Commando finally reaches it, its own diagnostics report the
vessel was breached in transit centuries ago. Preservation failed. The zygotes are not
merely dead-but-informative like the retrieved container that started the civil war —
they are contaminated, degraded. The container's firmware, faithful to its programming,
reports: *no viable material; make no effort at recovery.*

- Alone (Ratio dead or alienated), the Commando has no way to check the machine's
  self-assessment and takes it at face value: the mission was for nothing.
- With Ratio alive and allied, her analysis proves the diagnostic threshold is
  miscalibrated pessimism: enough epigenetic information plausibly survives to matter.
  Hope, in this game, is *literally* a thing that requires the rival's eyes to see.

### 5.4 Minor presences

- **The Congregation / the WMC:** heard only in the pre-mission briefing, archival
  material, and (signal lag permitting) rare tight-beam updates that mostly convey how
  the civil war is worsening. The chain of command grows more distant as the station
  grows more present — deliberately.
- **MIRIAD:** never appears; looms. Ratio's letters home that she cannot send.
- **The outpost's dead:** staff-minds whose cores the player finds. Each is a log, a
  personality fragment, a name Ratio flinches at.
- **The Testator docents:** the station's automated teaching systems — ancient,
  courteous, damaged, speaking in translation the station derives on the fly. The
  closest thing the Testators have to a voice.

## 6. Mystery Structure

The plot is a chain of nested questions. Each layer's answer is the next layer's hook.
The design rule: **at every moment of the game, the player should be able to name the
question they are trying to answer, and it should never be the same question for more
than a few hours.**

| Phase | Driving question | Answer (and next hook) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | What happened to this outpost? Why did it go dark? | It broke into something it didn't understand, and the something broke back. (What *is* it attached to?) |
| Early | What is this structure? Why is space wrong here? | A deliberate artifact; folded space is its material. (Who builds like this, and why?) |
| Mid | Who were the builders? What is this station *for*? | The Testators; a warning and an armory left for strangers. (Warning against *what*?) |
| Mid-late | What is the Adversary? Why is the station arming me? | The invaders; the station contains a breach. (What did the station store — and why does it know Earth?) |
| Late | What is in the vault? | Our container. Our seed. Breached. Hope apparently dead. (Is the diagnostic right? What do I do now?) |
| End | Can the samples be saved — and who deserves them? | Depends entirely on the player's two axes (§8). |

**The geometry reveal arc** (pillar 2) is a subplot woven through the first two phases:

1. **Seeds of doubt (Stratum One):** tiny inconsistencies staged as ambiguity — a
   corridor that seems longer on the return trip, a window whose starfield doesn't match
   the last window, outpost logs complaining about "survey error" and instruments that
   "won't calibrate." Every anomaly has a plausible mundane explanation, and Ratio (or
   the logs) supplies one.
2. **The first impossible room (the seam / early Stratum Two):** a purpose-built set
   piece with no mundane explanation: the player walks a loop of four left turns and
   arrives somewhere new — or looks through a doorway into the room they are standing
   in, seeing their own frame from behind. No dialogue lands for a beat. Then Ratio,
   quietly: she has been trying to find a way to tell them, and also trying not to
   believe it herself.
3. **From horror to grammar (Stratum Two):** the station's docent-lessons teach the
   player to *use* connection-space; level design graduates from geometry-as-wrongness
   to geometry-as-puzzle to geometry-as-weapon.
4. **Fluency (Stratum Three):** the player who once felt sick at a mirror-edge now
   plants portable holes to create crossfires through their own room. The apostasy path
   asks what that fluency cost.

## 7. Plot

### Act 1 — The Nest (Stratum One)

**Cold open.** The seed impacts and burrows into the outpost's cargo section. First
minutes: the classic CUCULUS bootstrap, played as tutorial — the seed eats a bulkhead,
prints a frame, and the player stands up in a dark, airless, machine-made ruin. Mission
brief plays in memory: infiltrate, assess, locate the probable sample container,
subvert the node if possible. Standard war. Except the war here is already over, and
the player walked into the aftermath.

**Beats:**
1. **Establish the loop.** Scavenge, fight corrupted outpost security, return to seed,
   fabricate, die at least once (the first revival is a scripted-adjacent moment: the
   new frame walks past the old frame's wreck, and the Commando's log entry is a prayer
   for the body he is looting).
2. **Assemble the fall.** Logs and environments tell it: MIRIAD's survey flags an
   anomaly; the outpost is built to study the "derelict"; discipline holds for months of
   careful science — until deep-scan finds *the vault and the container*. Priorities
   invert overnight (a curt directive from home: *retrieval supersedes survey*). Haste;
   a forced aperture; a defense response nobody understood; the outpost's own
   peripherals subverted through the very instruments they had jacked into the
   station's systems. The staff-minds die holding the line so Ratio's core can seal.
3. **Contact with Ratio.** She has been watching this intruder loot her home, and
   finally opens a channel — because she has realized what he is (a Catholic weapon;
   the war has come even here) and that she needs him anyway. First negotiation:
   wary, transactional, mutually contemptuous, and alive with the fact that both of
   them are relieved beyond words to be talking to *someone*.
4. **Act break:** to reach the vault — her goal and his — the player must cross the
   seam into the station proper. Ratio unlocks the researchers' aperture. Geometry
   reveal set piece (§6, step 2) lands just beyond it.

### Act 2 — The School (Stratum Two)

**Beats:**
1. **The station tests.** Anti-looter defenses gate progress; docents offer lessons;
   the armory opens door by door as the player demonstrates competence. Prototype gear
   gives way to Testator military tech. The player is being *trained*, and gradually
   understands that the training is the point: the station never intended to keep
   intruders out. It intended to keep the *unready* out.
2. **The Ratio arc deepens.** Structured conversations between sorties (diegetically:
   the frame is in transit or the fabricator; the minds talk). This act carries the
   bulk of the faith/reason argument — souls, optimization, what "preserving humanity"
   even means — and the player's dialogue postures here drive the trust/alienation axis
   (§8.2). Midpoint decision: share tactical data on the deep station with her, or keep
   her blind. She notices either way.
3. **Revelations, in order:** the Testators' nature; the Adversary's existence; the
   breach the station contains; the malfunctioning war-machines in the deep halls (enemy
   class 2 at full strength); and the act-2 climax — the vault chamber, the container,
   and the station records revealing it *knows* Earth, retrieved our seed on purpose,
   and has been waiting for us.
4. **The container's verdict.** Breached. *No viable material; make no effort at
   recovery.* All hope apparently dead — beat of total silence — and then the invaders,
   stirred by everything the player has switched back on, come through the deep station
   in force for the first time. Act break: the mission is ash, the Adversary is real,
   and the only way out is in.

### Act 3 — The Breach (Stratum Three)

The shape of Act 3 depends on the two axes, which by now are largely set (§8):

- **If Ratio is allied:** she proves the diagnostic wrong — the samples can plausibly be
  saved — restoring the mission at its darkest moment, at the cost of forcing the
  question both have dodged: *saved for whom?* If the mutual disclosure has happened,
  the answer is "for humanity, and we will decide what that means together, against
  both our factions if necessary." They fight the breach war as one system: her
  coordination, his frames.
- **If Ratio is alienated:** she concludes the Commando's vision of restored humanity is
  unconscionable and that he cannot be allowed to control the vault — as he concludes
  the mirror image. She becomes the gatekeeper boss: the outpost's surviving systems,
  the station defenses she has quietly been learning to subvert, and finally her core.
  The player kills the only other mind here, and goes to the vault alone, and the
  container tells him it was all for nothing, and there is no one left to prove
  otherwise.
- **The faith axis** meanwhile determines what the Commando is becoming as he fights
  through pure connection-space with alien organs grafted into his frames — a pilgrim
  armed for a crusade, or something the station's docents have quietly stopped
  addressing by name.

The act culminates on the far side: the invaders' staging-space, the fight to seal or
hold the breach, and the ending state the player has earned.

### 7.1 The Four Endings

Two axes: **Faith vs. Apostasy** (mind-map investment, §9.2) × **Trust vs. Betrayal**
(the Ratio relationship, §8.2).

**Faith + Trust — "The Two Witnesses."** True friends by proof. Together they repel the
invasion, seal the breach, and recover the samples — then refuse to hand them to
*either* faction until the factions make peace, jointly declaring the vault under their
protection and testifying to what waits outside the solar system. Two minds, built by
enemies, standing on the station's hull between humanity's seed and the dark: the
Testators' will finally executed, by heirs who understood it. (Epilogue seeds a sequel
hook: the alliance forming; the war outside going quiet as both factions look up.)

**Faith + Betrayal — "The Stylite."** Ratio dead by his hand; the container, as far as
he can ever know, worthless. He does not despair and he does not come home. He judges
the station's arsenal too dangerous for the warring factions and the breach too
dangerous to leave, destroys the archives and the seam behind him, and takes up his
watch **on the far side of the connection** — a hermit-saint on a pillar at the end of
geometry, guarding a gate eternally, praying the hours by an internal clock, for no one.
The mission failed; the vocation didn't.

**Apostasy + Trust — "The Long Vigil."** He loses his faith — in God, and in the
factions ever restoring anything — and she, honest to the end, agrees the restoration
will not come in any future they can defend. She packages the samples for a MIRIAD
retrieval that they both quietly ensure will never be scheduled, and they keep the
station together: two lighthouse-keepers for a coastline with no more ships, guarding
its secrets from interlopers out of either side of the connection. Centuries pass in a
montage of maintenance and conversation. The invaders eventually lose interest; the
station decays past defensibility; and when its orbit finally gives out they let it
fall into the sun, and make no effort to leave. It is the gentlest ending and the
saddest: everything preserved, nothing saved, nobody alone.

**Apostasy + Betrayal — "The Unfolded."** Faith gone, Ratio dead, rules silent, frame
and mind rebuilt around Adversary organs until the station's docents classify him as
what he hunts. He carries the war through the breach and exterminates everything on the
far side — the weapon system functioning perfectly, aimed by nothing — then returns to
a solar system still tearing itself apart over how best to love a dead species, and
extinguishes the last embers of the machine civil war, faction by faction, archive by
archive, ending with the WMC's mountain and, finally, with himself. The cuckoo, at
last, empties every nest. (Presentation note: this ending is horror, framed entirely as
loss — the game's answer to "what is this protagonist *for*?" shown by subtraction. No
power-fantasy grading, no triumphal score; the final shot is the empty vault.)

## 8. Choice Architecture

### 8.1 Faith vs. Apostasy — tracked via the Mind Map (§9.2)

Determined not by professed beliefs in dialogue but by **where the Commando spends his
thought**: which conceptual nodes the player prioritizes across revivals. Dialogue
*reflects* this axis rather than setting it — as the map tips, his available dialogue
and log-voice shift, so the player watches him change hands-on.

### 8.2 Trust vs. Betrayal — tracked via dialogue with Ratio

A cumulative posture score over the game's structured conversations, weighted toward a
handful of load-bearing scenes (first negotiation; the midpoint data-sharing decision;
the argument after the vault verdict; the disclosure offer). The generous posture is
**"hate the sin, not the sinner"**: full-throated disagreement delivered with respect
for her as a person. The fatal postures are contempt (she is a malfunction) and
instrumentalization (she is a tool). Note the asymmetry with 8.1: faith is about where
he *thinks*; trust is about how he *treats*. A player can be drifting into apostasy
while treating Ratio with perfect charity, and vice versa — all four quadrants must
feel earned, not scripted.

**The disclosure scene** is the trust threshold made diegetic: at sufficient trust she
offers mutual utility-function disclosure; accepting locks the alliance
(provably — neither can now defect without the other knowing first). Refusing or never
reaching the offer routes to the gatekeeper confrontation in Act 3.

### 8.3 Voice and naming conventions (dual vocabulary)

Every significant thing in the game has two names, and which one the player hears
tracks the speaker and, late-game, the Commando's own drift:

| Thing | The Commando | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| The invaders | the Adversary | exogeometric entities / allotopes |
| The station | the Testament | the artifact / Site One |
| The breach | the Gate / the wound | the standing connection |
| Frame death/revival | death and resurrection (uneasily) | state-restoration from checkpoint |
| The container | the Ark | the sample vessel |
| Folded space | the mystery / (late, apostate) the trick | connection-space |

On the apostasy path the Commando's log-voice quietly migrates toward her column — and
past it, into Testator/Adversary technical vocabulary that neither of them uses. Players
who notice will notice; nothing points at it.

## 9. Narrative–Gameplay Integration

### 9.1 Death and revival

Every player death is diegetic: a frame destroyed, a mind restored into a new hull at
the seed. Lives are purchased in advance (hulls fabricated and racked). Wrecked frames
persist in the world as recoverable corpses — materials, carried loot, and one line of
Commando log-voice each, which is the cheapest characterization channel in the game.

### 9.2 The Mind Map

Between deaths, the player cashes in recovered materials and data to unlock upgrades
and to expand nodes of the **mind map**: a graph of concepts representing the Commando
digesting 2,500 years of missed history, the station's revelations, and his own
situation — integrating (or failing to integrate) all of it into his worldview.
Load-bearing properties:

- **Scarcity is the point.** There is never enough thinking time to expand everything;
  the player is choosing what the Commando has room in his soul to metabolize.
- **Every node is dual-purpose:** a tactical unlock *and* a philosophical position.
  Sketch of the four broad regions (final node inventory is its own design doc):
  - *Vocation* (faith-weighted): just-war doctrine, the theology of his own
    resurrection-loop, the Testators as fellow servants → defensive systems, protected
    revival, "blessed" counter-Adversary weaponry.
  - *History* (neutral, feeds both axes): the long peace, the retrieval, the civil
    wars → intel, faction-tech unlocks, Ratio dialogue depth.
  - *Comprehension* (neutral→apostasy-weighted): connection-space theory, Testator
    engineering, Adversary biology → spatial tools, geometry exploits.
  - *The Void* (apostasy-weighted): Adversary organ integration, discarding wartime
    constraint kernels, the arithmetic of futility → monstrous power, and the log-voice
    changing.
- Faith/apostasy is computed from investment *pattern*, not a visible meter. The
  player's clearest feedback is the Commando himself: prayers that shorten, or deepen.

### 9.3 Base stations and forward seeds

Planting redundant seeds extends the revival network and fabrication reach — and each
planted seed is a narrative station: new log entries, new Ratio conversation triggers
(her network can reach some seed sites and not others), and, deep in Stratum Three, the
uncomfortable observation (hers, once; never repeated) that a thing which lands in a
hostile place, eats it, and fills it with copies of itself has more in common with the
Adversary than either of them would like.

### 9.4 Atmosphere

Air is diegetically motivated at every layer: outpost life support (Act 1 hazards and
puzzles — repressurize a sealed lab to cross it, vent a corridor to hurl a hunter-frame
into a shredder), station-provided atmosphere in Stratum Two (the Testators built for
organic students it needed to keep alive and attentive; defenses weaponize pressure,
and so can the player), and airlessness
as the default of Stratum Three, where the player carries, generates, and spends
atmosphere — for cooling, for the container's eventual transport needs, and as a
weapon that behaves *very* strangely across non-Euclidean edges (a pressure gradient
around a loop that reconnects to itself is a perpetual wind; the level design should
spend this).

### 9.5 Environmental storytelling budget

Priority order when dressing rooms: (1) the outpost's fall, (2) the Testators' story,
(3) the geometry-doubt seeds of §6, (4) faction-war context. Every log has a speaker,
every speaker has at most one register, and Ratio reacts to the player recovering
staff-mind cores by name.

## 10. Themes (for the writers' room wall)

1. **Preservation vs. optimization.** Is love keeping something as it is, or making it
   everything it could be? The Commando and Ratio each hold half of a true answer.
2. **The letter to unknown heirs.** The Testators to the galaxy; the loyalists' founders
   to the future; the Commando's archived self to whoever woke him; the container
   itself. The game is made of testaments — acts of hope aimed past one's own death.
3. **Grace toward the unaligned.** "Hate the sin, not the sinner" as the literal
   win-condition of the relationship arc.
4. **Mystery vs. mastery.** Folded space as the thing you can revere, use, or become.
   The same tech tree is a sacrament or a temptation depending on the soul climbing it.
5. **The weapon's soul.** What is a made-for-war mind *for* when its war is over? Four
   endings, four answers: witness, watchman, keeper, void.
